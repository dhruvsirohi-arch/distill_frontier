Technical report · Latency characterization

A vision-language-action model that acts in ten milliseconds.

A 39.7 M-parameter VLA turns two camera frames and an instruction into a 16-step action chunk in about 10 ms on an RTX 4090, and under 7 ms once compiled. Published figures for generalist VLAs range from 27 ms to over 300 ms; those models are 30 to 1,400 times larger and do far more.

Abstract

We time every stage of action generation for a small VLA that places a named bowl on a named moving pad. Inference is tight around 10 ms with a short tail, the action head costs more than the vision encoder, and three settings cut latency further with no measured loss of success. The model sits in the latency class of the fast layer in two-level robot stacks, at a fraction of a generalist model's capability.

01 · Setup

What was measured

The model takes two 224×224 camera images, six joint positions and a text instruction, and outputs joint targets for a 6-DoF arm at 25 Hz. One inference predicts 16 control ticks (0.64 s); by default the first 4 are executed, then the model replans.

01

Upload

Two uint8 frames and the joint state are copied to the GPU.

0.20 ms
02

See

DINOv2-S encodes the two new frames. Tokens for the earlier frame are cached.

2.76 ms · 22.1 M params
03

Fuse

A 4-layer transformer mixes 281 image, text and joint tokens.

1.13 ms · 7.1 M params
04

Act

Four flow-matching steps refine a 16-tick action chunk, then it returns to the CPU.

5.68 ms · 9.5 M params

"Latency" is the wall-clock time of all four stages. Every timing brackets the work with torch.cuda.synchronize(), at batch size 1, with nothing else on the GPU. Camera capture, rendering and actuation are excluded. The instruction is encoded once by a frozen CLIP text model and cached, so it adds nothing per tick.

02 · Distribution

Tight around 10 ms, with a short tail

Across 100 closed-loop episodes the model ran 3,329 inferences. Ninety percent fall between 9.9 and 10.8 ms. Five took longer than 12 ms; the slowest took 18.2 ms, under half of the 40 ms control tick.

03 · Breakdown

More than half the time is the action head

The vision encoder is not the bottleneck. Four flow-matching steps take 5.6 ms, about 1.4 ms each, for a 9.5 M-parameter decoder working on 16 tokens.

At this size the cost is per-call overhead, not arithmetic: encoding four images takes the same 2.5 ms as encoding two. That is why the remedies in the next section are about removing overhead, not shrinking the model.

The instruction never enters this path as text. A new sentence takes 3.0 ms to encode once, so changing the instruction costs a dictionary lookup plus the next ordinary inference.

04 · Settings

Three settings cut latency, none cost accuracy

Fewer flow steps, full precision and compilation each help. Task success stayed at 100% (50 episodes per setting) through all of them, down to a single flow step.

The two uncompiled lines are timed on recorded frames; the compiled line is from closed-loop runs, which read about 0.3 ms higher. Compiled runs were not measured at 8 steps.

bf16 is slower than fp32

10.2 against 8.0 ms in closed loop. The autocast casts cost more than half-width arithmetic saves at this size. bf16 was chosen for training and left on.

Compilation removes overhead

6.7 ms at 4 steps and 4.5 ms at 1 step, for 5 to 15 s of warm-up at start.

CPU only: 65 ms

On the i9-14900K with 24 threads in fp32. Too slow to replan every tick, enough to replan every 2 ticks.

05 · Reaction time

How often it replans sets the real delay

Inference latency is not the delay the robot experiences. With action chunking, the last action executed from a chunk was computed from an observation several ticks old: up to 130 ms when replanning every 4 ticks, 10 ms when replanning every tick.

"Oldest observation acted on" is (interval − 1) × 40 ms plus the inference time. Inference is slower at 8 and 16 ticks because the earlier frame is no longer in the token cache and is encoded in a second call.

The pads move at 1 to 3 cm/s, slow enough that even open-loop chunks mostly succeed; a faster target would separate these rows. At 10 ms per inference the model could replan at up to about 90 Hz, so the control rate is limited by the 25 Hz simulation setting, not by the model.

It shows a latency class

At 7 to 10 ms this model sits with the fast "System 1" layer of two-level designs. Figure's Helix reports an 80 M-parameter S1 emitting actions at 200 Hz under a 7 B-parameter S2 at 7 to 9 Hz; GR00T N1 describes a 10 Hz VLM under a 120 Hz action module. Neither publishes a per-inference S1 latency, so they are in the table but not on the chart.

It does not show equal capability

This model knows two bowl colours and two pad colours in one simulated scene. π0, GR00T and OpenVLA handle open vocabulary, many embodiments and real camera images. Their latency pays for a multi-billion-parameter language backbone that this model does not have.

The numbers share no protocol

Hardware ranges from a Jetson to an A100, image counts from one to three, and chunk lengths from 1 to 63. None of the other models were re-run here.

Throughput flatters chunked models

Several sources quote "Hz" as chunk length divided by latency (π0 at 291 Hz, for example). That is how fast actions can be played back, not how fast a model reacts. By that definition this model produces 1,550 actions per second; the honest reaction figure is 10 ms.

07 · Implications

For a reasoning layer on top

Latency is not the constraint

The model uses 6.6% of one GPU at 25 Hz. A slower reasoning model can share the device or run remotely, and only needs to send a sentence.

New instructions land in one tick

set_instruction() clears the queued actions, so the next tick replans: 3 ms to encode a new sentence, then one inference. 30 of 30 mid-carry changes ended at the newly named pad.

The cheapest speed-up is unused

Compiling and dropping to 2 flow steps gives 5.3 ms with no measured loss, and would allow replanning every tick at 13% GPU duty.

08 · Limitations

What these numbers do not cover

  • Inference time is not fed back into the simulation. The simulator waits while the model computes, so an action is applied to the state it was computed from. On a real robot the world moves during those 10 ms; at these pad speeds, 0.3 mm.
  • The headline assumes a hot loop. All figures except Table 1 come from back-to-back inference. Paced with a plain sleep, the median rises to 23 ms on this machine.
  • One GPU, one machine, one day. No thermal soak and no contention from other processes. The 99.9th percentile rests on three samples.
  • No camera or actuation latency. Real cameras add tens of milliseconds, which would dominate this model's share.
  • Sweep rows use 50 episodes. "100%" there means 50 of 50; a difference of 2 to 3 points between settings would not be visible.
  • The task is narrow. Fewer flow steps and longer chunks are free here partly because the motion is slow and smooth.

Run it yourself

# code, configs and training commands are on the vla-mujoco branch (see RESULTS.md)
git clone -b vla-mujoco git@github.com:dhruvsirohi-arch/distill_frontier.git
cd distill_frontier && uv sync --frozen

.venv/bin/python -m vla.latency          --ckpt outputs/vla/runs/m2_early/best.pt
.venv/bin/python -m vla.latency_variants --ckpt outputs/vla/runs/m2_early/best.pt
