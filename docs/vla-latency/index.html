Action-generation latency of a small VLA for real-time control

A 39.7 M-parameter vision-language-action model produces a 16-step action chunk in about 10 ms on an RTX 4090, and in under 7 ms once compiled. Published figures for generalist VLAs range from 27 ms to over 300 ms per inference; those models are 30 to 1,400 times larger and do far more.

What was measured

The model takes two 224×224 camera images, six joint positions and a text instruction, and outputs joint position targets for a 6-DoF arm at 25 Hz. The task is to place a named bowl on a named moving pad in MuJoCo. One inference predicts 16 control ticks (0.64 s) of actions; by default the first 4 are executed and then the model replans.

"Latency" here is the wall-clock time of one inference: copy the frames to the GPU, encode them, fuse with the cached instruction embedding and joint state, run the flow-matching action head, and copy the actions back to the CPU. Every timing brackets the work with torch.cuda.synchronize(), at batch size 1, with nothing else on the GPU. Camera capture, rendering and actuation are excluded.

Latency is tight around 10 ms, with a short tail

Across 100 closed-loop episodes the model ran 3,329 inferences. Ninety percent fall between 9.9 and 10.8 ms. Five took longer than 12 ms, and the slowest took 18.2 ms, still under half of the 40 ms control tick.

The very first inference after loading the model is the exception: 175 ms, as CUDA kernels initialize. Loading the checkpoint takes 2.4 s. A deployment should run one throw-away inference before the control loop starts.

More than half of the time is the action head

The vision encoder (DINOv2-S on two images) is not the bottleneck. The four flow-matching steps take 5.6 ms, about 1.4 ms each, for a 9.5 M-parameter decoder working on 16 tokens. At this size the cost is per-call overhead, not arithmetic: encoding four images takes the same 2.5 ms as encoding two.

The instruction never enters this path as text. It is encoded once by a frozen CLIP text model (3.0 ms for a new sentence, then cached), so changing the instruction costs one dictionary lookup plus the next ordinary inference.

Three settings trade latency, none of them cost accuracy here

Fewer flow steps, full precision and compilation each cut latency, and task success stayed at 100% (50 episodes per setting) through all of them, down to a single flow step.

The two uncompiled lines are timed on recorded frames; the compiled line is from closed-loop runs, which read about 0.3 ms higher. Compiled runs were not measured at 8 steps.

  • bf16 is slower than fp32 at this size (10.2 against 8.0 ms in closed loop). The autocast casts cost more than the half-width arithmetic saves. bf16 was chosen for training and left on by habit.
  • torch.compile removes most of the per-call overhead: 6.7 ms at 4 steps, 4.5 ms at 1 step. It costs 5 to 15 s of warm-up at start.
  • On the CPU alone (i9-14900K, 24 threads, fp32) one inference takes 65 ms. That is too slow to replan every tick but enough to replan every 2 ticks.

How often to replan sets the real reaction time

Inference latency is not the delay the robot experiences. With action chunking, the last action executed from a chunk was computed from an observation several ticks old. Replanning every 4 ticks, that observation is up to 130 ms old; replanning every tick it is 10 ms old and uses a quarter of the GPU.

"Oldest observation acted on" is (interval − 1) × 40 ms plus the inference time. Inference is slower at 8 and 16 ticks because the earlier frame is no longer in the token cache and is encoded in a second call.

The pads here move at 1 to 3 cm/s, which is slow enough that even open-loop chunks mostly succeed. A faster target would separate these rows. At 10 ms per inference the model could replan at up to about 90 Hz, so the control rate is limited by the 25 Hz simulation setting, not by the model.

What the comparison does and does not show

  • It shows a latency class. At 7 to 10 ms this model sits with the fast "System 1" layer of two-level designs, not with single large VLAs. Figure's Helix reports an 80 M-parameter S1 emitting actions at 200 Hz under a 7 B-parameter S2 running at 7 to 9 Hz; GR00T N1 describes a 10 Hz VLM under a 120 Hz action module. Neither publishes a per-inference S1 latency, so they are in the table but not on the chart.
  • It does not show equal capability. This model knows two bowl colours and two pad colours in one simulated scene. π0, GR00T and OpenVLA handle open vocabulary, many embodiments and real camera images. Their latency pays for a multi-billion-parameter language backbone that this model does not have.
  • The numbers are not measured under one protocol. Hardware ranges from a Jetson to an A100, image counts from one to three, and chunk sizes from 1 to 63. Only this work's rows were measured here. None of the other models were re-run here.
  • Throughput figures flatter chunked models. Several sources quote "Hz" as chunk size divided by latency (π0 at 291 Hz, for example). That is how fast actions can be played back, not how fast the model reacts. By that definition this model produces 1,550 actions per second; the honest reaction figure is the 10 ms above.

What this means for a reasoning layer on top

  • Latency budget is not the constraint. The model uses 6.6% of one GPU at 25 Hz. A slower reasoning model can share the device or run remotely, and only needs to send a sentence.
  • An instruction change takes effect within one chunk boundary. set_instruction() clears the queued actions, so the next tick replans: about 3 ms to encode a new sentence, then one 10 ms inference. In the benchmark, 30 of 30 mid-carry instruction changes ended at the newly named pad.
  • The cheapest speed-up is unused. Compiling and dropping to 2 flow steps gives 5.3 ms with no measured loss, and would allow replanning every tick at 13% GPU duty.

Limitations

  • Inference time is not fed back into the simulation. The simulator waits while the model thinks, so an action is applied to the same state it was computed from. On a real robot the world moves during those 10 ms; at these pad speeds that is 0.3 mm.
  • One GPU, one machine, one day. No thermal soak, no contention from other processes, and the 99.9th percentile rests on three samples.
  • No camera or actuation latency. Real cameras add tens of milliseconds, which would dominate this model's share.
  • Sweep rows use 50 episodes. "100%" there means 50 of 50; a 2 to 3 point difference between settings would not be visible.
  • Success is on a narrow task. Fewer flow steps and longer chunks are free here partly because the motion is slow and smooth.
